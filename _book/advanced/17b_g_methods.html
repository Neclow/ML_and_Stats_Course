24  Estimating Treatment Effects: Inverse Probability Weighting and G-Computation

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Imagine you are handed a hospital database and a single, deceptively simple question: does this blood-pressure drug actually prevent deaths, or does it only look protective because the doctors who prescribe it tend to pick their healthier patients? In the previous chapter we met this problem — confounding by indication — and we learned to draw it out with a directed acyclic graph (DAG), to estimate a propensity score, and to balance groups by matching. This chapter takes the next step: once the groups are balanced, how do we actually compute the treatment effect, with an honest confidence interval, in a way that recovers the truth? Two methods do almost all the heavy lifting in modern causal analysis, and both come from the work of the epidemiologist James Robins. They are called the g-methods (“g” for generalised). This chapter is a slow, hands-on tour of the two foundational ones: inverse probability weighting and g-computation.

24.1 Introduction

In the last chapter we built the conceptual scaffolding. We said that every patient has two potential outcomes — what would happen if treated, and what would happen if untreated — and that the causal effect is the contrast between them. We also said that the central obstacle is confounding: the treated and untreated patients differ systematically, so a raw comparison is biased. We introduced the propensity score (a patient’s probability of being treated given their characteristics) and used it to match patients and, briefly, to weight them.

This chapter answers the practical question that follows: given a balanced or balanceable dataset, how do I produce a single, defensible number for the average treatment effect? There are two foundational answers, and it is worth knowing both because they make their assumptions in different places:

  • Inverse probability weighting (IPW) models who gets treated. It builds a re-weighted “pseudo-population” in which treatment is no longer tied to the confounders, then compares outcomes in that pseudo-population.
  • G-computation (also called the parametric g-formula or standardisation) models what the outcome would be. It fits an outcome model, then uses it to predict every patient’s outcome twice — once as if treated, once as if untreated — and contrasts the averages.

Both target the same quantity, the average treatment effect (ATE) we defined last chapter, and under the same core assumption (no unmeasured confounding) both recover it. We will build each from first principles, run them on simulated data where we know the right answer, and show that a naive regression gets it wrong while the g-methods get it right.

ImportantWhy two methods instead of one?

A naive analyst fits one regression and reads off a coefficient. The trouble is that a regression coefficient for treatment is only the causal effect under restrictive conditions (no interactions, correct functional form, a collapsible effect measure). The g-methods sidestep these traps by computing the effect the way the definition demands — as a contrast of average potential outcomes — rather than trusting a single coefficient to mean what we hope it means. And because IPW and g-computation lean on different models, agreement between them is itself reassuring evidence.

24.2 The Running Clinical Example

Throughout this chapter we use one scenario. We have observational data on patients started (or not) on a hypothetical drug, and we want its effect on one-year mortality. The catch, as always, is confounding by indication: sicker patients — older, with worse kidney function, more comorbidity — are both more likely to be treated and more likely to die regardless of treatment. A raw comparison will therefore make the drug look useless or even harmful, even if it genuinely saves lives. Our job is to undo that distortion.

The single arrow from drug to mortality is the causal effect we want. The two arrows out of illness severity are the backdoor path that contaminates the raw comparison. Everything below is machinery for shutting that backdoor.

24.3 Inverse Probability Weighting

24.3.1 The idea in plain language

Recall the propensity score from last chapter: each patient’s probability of receiving the treatment they were eligible for, given their measured characteristics (Haukoos and Lewis 2015). Inverse probability of treatment weighting (often shortened to IPW or IPTW) uses that score to re-weight the sample.

Here is the intuition, set out accessibly for clinical readers by Mansournia and Altman (2016) and Chesnaye et al. (2022). Suppose a very sick patient — the kind clinicians almost always treat — nevertheless went untreated. That patient is rare and precious: they are one of the few untreated people who look like the treated group, so they carry a lot of information about what happens to treated-type patients when they go without the drug. IPW gives such a patient a large weight. Conversely, an untreated patient who looked very unlikely to be treated anyway is “over-represented” among controls, so IPW shrinks their weight. Do this for everyone and you build a pseudo-population in which being treated is statistically unrelated to the confounders — exactly the situation a randomised trial creates by design.

The weight for each patient is the inverse of the probability of the treatment they actually received:

\[ w_i = \frac{A_i}{e(L_i)} + \frac{1 - A_i}{1 - e(L_i)} \]

where \(A_i\) is 1 if treated and 0 if not, and \(e(L_i)\) is the propensity score. A treated patient with propensity score 0.2 gets weight \(1/0.2 = 5\); an untreated patient with propensity score 0.9 (so probability of being untreated \(= 0.1\)) gets weight \(1/0.1 = 10\). These weights are larger precisely for the informative, “surprising” patients.

24.3.2 Stabilised weights

The raw weights above can occasionally become enormous — if a treated patient had a propensity score of 0.01, their weight is 100, and a single patient can then dominate the analysis and inflate the variance. Stabilised weights tame this by replacing the “1” in the numerator with the overall probability of receiving that treatment:

\[ sw_i = \frac{A_i \cdot P(A=1)}{e(L_i)} + \frac{(1 - A_i) \cdot P(A=0)}{1 - e(L_i)} \]

The estimate is unchanged on average, but the weights cluster sensibly around 1 and the confidence intervals are narrower and more honest. Stabilised weights are the default recommendation and what modern packages produce automatically.

NoteMarginal structural models in one line

When you fit an outcome model (say, a regression of mortality on treatment) using these weights, the model you have fitted is called a marginal structural model (MSM). “Marginal” because it describes the whole population (not conditional on confounders), and “structural” because its coefficients have a causal interpretation. MSMs are the natural home of IPW and the standard tool when a treatment changes over time — a setting where ordinary regression genuinely cannot give the right answer.

24.3.3 Checking the weights worked: balance and positivity

Weighting is only trustworthy if it actually balanced the confounders, and only valid if every kind of patient could in principle have received either treatment. So two checks are mandatory:

  1. Covariate balance. After weighting, the treated and control groups should have a near-identical mix of confounders. We measure this with the standardised mean difference (SMD) — the difference in a covariate’s mean between groups, divided by its standard deviation — and want every value comfortably below 0.1.
  2. Positivity. Recall from last chapter: every patient must have a non-zero chance of either treatment. In weighting terms, a propensity score near 0 or 1 produces an explosive weight. So we inspect the largest weights; a maximum weight above roughly 10–20 is a warning sign that positivity is shaky and the estimate may be unstable.

24.3.4 IPW in practice

  • R
  • Python
Code 
library(WeightIt)        # estimate balancing weights
library(cobalt)          # check covariate balance
library(marginaleffects) # turn the weighted model into an effect

# --- Simulate a confounded cohort with a KNOWN true effect ---
set.seed(2024)
n <- 3000
age       <- rnorm(n, 65, 10)
egfr      <- rnorm(n, 60, 15)               # kidney function
comorbid  <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.4)              # any major comorbidity

# Sicker patients (older, worse kidneys, comorbid) are MORE likely treated
lp_treat  <- -1 + 0.03 * (age - 65) - 0.03 * (egfr - 60) + 0.5 * comorbid
ps_true   <- plogis(lp_treat)
treat     <- rbinom(n, 1, ps_true)

# TRUE causal effect of treatment on mortality: log-odds -0.7 (protective)
lp_death  <- -1 + 0.04 * (age - 65) - 0.03 * (egfr - 60) + 0.6 * comorbid - 0.7 * treat
death     <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(lp_death))

dat <- data.frame(age, egfr, comorbid, treat, death)

# --- Step 1: estimate stabilised weights for the ATE ---
W <- weightit(treat ~ age + egfr + comorbid,
              data = dat, method = "glm", estimand = "ATE",
              stabilize = TRUE)

# --- Step 2: check balance and the size of the weights ---
bal.tab(W, thresholds = c(m = 0.1))   # SMDs after weighting; want < 0.1
summary(W)                            # inspect the largest weights (positivity)

# --- Step 3: fit the weighted outcome model (a marginal structural model) ---
msm <- glm_weightit(death ~ treat, data = dat, weightit = W,
                    family = binomial)

# --- Step 4: read off the causal effect on the risk-difference scale ---
avg_comparisons(msm, variables = "treat")

What the code shows. We first manufacture a cohort where treatment is deliberately confounded and the true protective effect is built in (the - 0.7 * treat term in the mortality equation). weightit() estimates the propensity score and converts it into stabilised ATE weights in one call. bal.tab() then prints a table of standardised mean differences for age, egfr, and comorbid after weighting — you want every number under 0.1, confirming the pseudo-population is balanced. summary(W) reports the range of weights; a sane maximum (well under 20 here) means positivity is not violated. Finally glm_weightit() fits the weighted outcome model — this is the marginal structural model — and avg_comparisons() converts it into a clean risk difference: the average change in the probability of death caused by the drug, with a confidence interval that correctly accounts for the weighting. Because we hard-wired a protective effect, you should see a negative risk difference (fewer deaths under treatment), unlike the naive analysis we run later.

Code 
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

# --- Simulate a confounded cohort with a KNOWN true effect ---
rng = np.random.default_rng(2024)
n = 3000
age      = rng.normal(65, 10, n)
egfr     = rng.normal(60, 15, n)
comorbid = rng.binomial(1, 0.4, n)

lp_treat = -1 + 0.03*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60) + 0.5*comorbid
ps_true  = 1/(1+np.exp(-lp_treat))
treat    = rng.binomial(1, ps_true)

# TRUE causal effect: log-odds -0.7 (protective)
lp_death = -1 + 0.04*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60) + 0.6*comorbid - 0.7*treat
death    = rng.binomial(1, 1/(1+np.exp(-lp_death)))

df = pd.DataFrame(dict(age=age, egfr=egfr, comorbid=comorbid,
                       treat=treat, death=death))

# --- Step 1: estimate the propensity score ---
ps_model = smf.logit("treat ~ age + egfr + comorbid", data=df).fit(disp=0)
df["ps"] = ps_model.predict(df)

# --- Step 2: stabilised weights ---
p_treat = df["treat"].mean()
df["sw"] = np.where(df["treat"] == 1,
                    p_treat / df["ps"],
                    (1 - p_treat) / (1 - df["ps"]))

# --- Step 3: check balance (weighted standardised mean differences) ---
def wsmd(v):
    t, c = df[df.treat == 1], df[df.treat == 0]
    mt = np.average(t[v], weights=t.sw)
    mc = np.average(c[v], weights=c.sw)
    sd = np.sqrt((t[v].var() + c[v].var()) / 2)
    return (mt - mc) / sd

print("Weighted SMDs (want |value| < 0.1):")
for v in ["age", "egfr", "comorbid"]:
    print(f"  {v}: {wsmd(v):+.3f}")
print("Max weight (positivity check):", df["sw"].max().round(2))

# --- Step 4: weighted outcome model on the risk-difference scale ---
msm = smf.glm("death ~ treat", data=df, family=sm.families.Binomial(),
              var_weights=df["sw"]).fit()
# Convert to a marginal risk difference by predicting under treat=0/1
d1 = df.assign(treat=1); d0 = df.assign(treat=0)
rd = (msm.predict(d1) - msm.predict(d0)).mean()
print(f"\nIPW risk difference (treat - control): {rd:+.4f}")

What the code shows. This is the same workflow written from scratch so every step is visible. We simulate the identical confounded cohort, fit a logistic propensity-score model with statsmodels, and build stabilised weights by hand. The wsmd helper prints the weighted standardised mean difference for each confounder — all should be near zero (under 0.1) if weighting balanced the groups — and we print the maximum weight as a positivity check. We then fit the weighted outcome model and, rather than reading a single coefficient, we predict each patient’s death probability twice (everyone treated, then everyone untreated) and average the difference. That average is the risk difference, directly comparable to the R output, and should be negative because we built in a protective effect.

24.4 G-Computation

24.4.1 The idea in plain language

IPW models the treatment. G-computation instead models the outcome, and it follows the definition of the average treatment effect almost literally.

Recall the definition: the ATE is the average outcome if everyone were treated minus the average outcome if everyone were untreated. We never observe both worlds for any patient — but we can predict them. The recipe has three steps:

  1. Fit one outcome model that includes treatment and all the confounders (and, ideally, their interactions). This model learns how outcome depends jointly on treatment and patient characteristics.
  2. Predict twice for every patient. First set everyone’s treatment to “treated” (leaving their real confounders untouched) and predict each patient’s outcome. Then reset everyone to “untreated” and predict again. Each patient now has two predicted outcomes — their estimated potential outcomes.
  3. Average and contrast. Average the “all treated” predictions, average the “all untreated” predictions, and take the difference. That difference is the average treatment effect.

This procedure is also called standardisation, because it standardises both arms to the same confounder distribution (the whole sample’s). It is the oldest idea in the chapter — Robins introduced the g-formula in 1986 — and it remains one of the most reliable.

24.4.2 Confidence intervals: use the bootstrap

Because g-computation predicts, averages, and contrasts, the uncertainty in the final number does not come out of the outcome model directly. The standard solution is the bootstrap: resample the patients with replacement many times, rerun the whole predict-average-contrast procedure on each resample, and use the spread of the resulting estimates as the confidence interval. Modern tooling automates this — in R, marginaleffects::inferences() wraps the bootstrap around the whole calculation.

24.4.3 G-computation in practice

  • R
  • Python
Code 
library(marginaleffects)  # avg_comparisons() + inferences() bootstrap

# --- Simulate the SAME confounded cohort with a known true effect ---
set.seed(2024)
n <- 3000
age       <- rnorm(n, 65, 10)
egfr      <- rnorm(n, 60, 15)
comorbid  <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.4)
ps_true   <- plogis(-1 + 0.03*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60) + 0.5*comorbid)
treat     <- rbinom(n, 1, ps_true)
death     <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-1 + 0.04*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60) +
                                   0.6*comorbid - 0.7*treat))
dat <- data.frame(age, egfr, comorbid, treat, death)

# --- Step 1: fit ONE outcome model with treatment + confounders ---
#     Include treatment-confounder interactions so effects can vary by patient
out_model <- glm(death ~ treat * (age + egfr + comorbid),
                 data = dat, family = binomial)

# --- Steps 2-4: predict under treat=1 and treat=0, average, contrast ---
#     avg_comparisons does the standardisation; inferences() bootstraps the CI
avg_comparisons(out_model, variables = "treat") |>
  inferences(method = "boot", R = 500)

What the code shows. We rebuild the identical confounded cohort (this chunk is fully self-contained). We then fit a single logistic outcome model, deliberately including treat * (age + egfr + comorbid) so the drug’s effect is allowed to differ across patient types — a flexibility plain regression coefficients cannot capture. avg_comparisons(..., variables = "treat") carries out the g-computation automatically: it predicts every patient’s death probability with treat set to 1, again with it set to 0, averages each, and reports the difference as a risk difference. Piping into inferences(method = "boot", R = 500) wraps the whole procedure in a 500-replicate bootstrap so the confidence interval is honest. The estimate should be negative and close to the IPW estimate above — two different models, agreeing on the truth.

Code 
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

# --- Simulate the SAME confounded cohort ---
rng = np.random.default_rng(2024)
n = 3000
age      = rng.normal(65, 10, n)
egfr     = rng.normal(60, 15, n)
comorbid = rng.binomial(1, 0.4, n)
ps_true  = 1/(1+np.exp(-(-1 + 0.03*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60) + 0.5*comorbid)))
treat    = rng.binomial(1, ps_true)
death    = rng.binomial(1, 1/(1+np.exp(-(-1 + 0.04*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60)
                                          + 0.6*comorbid - 0.7*treat))))
df = pd.DataFrame(dict(age=age, egfr=egfr, comorbid=comorbid,
                       treat=treat, death=death))

# --- The g-computation procedure as a reusable function ---
def gcomp(data):
    m = smf.glm("death ~ treat * (age + egfr + comorbid)",
                data=data, family=sm.families.Binomial()).fit(disp=0)
    d1 = data.assign(treat=1)   # everyone treated
    d0 = data.assign(treat=0)   # everyone untreated
    return m.predict(d1).mean() - m.predict(d0).mean()

point = gcomp(df)

# --- Bootstrap the confidence interval ---
rng_boot = np.random.default_rng(1)
boot = np.array([
    gcomp(df.sample(len(df), replace=True, random_state=int(rng_boot.integers(1e9))))
    for _ in range(500)
])
lo, hi = np.percentile(boot, [2.5, 97.5])
print(f"G-computation risk difference: {point:+.4f}  (95% CI {lo:+.4f}, {hi:+.4f})")

What the code shows. We wrap the predict-average-contrast recipe in a function, gcomp, that fits the interaction outcome model, predicts every patient’s death probability under “everyone treated” and “everyone untreated”, and returns the average difference. Calling it once on the real data gives the point estimate. We then call it 500 more times on bootstrap resamples (sampling patients with replacement) and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of those estimates as a 95 percent confidence interval. The printed risk difference should be negative and close to the R g-computation result and the IPW result — demonstrating that, when the assumptions hold, the route you take to the answer matters less than getting the confounding adjustment right.

TipA ready-made alternative in R

You do not have to hand-roll the outcome model. The stdReg2 package implements standardisation (g-computation) directly with built-in confidence intervals, and for time-varying treatments — where a patient’s treatment and confounders evolve over follow-up, and ordinary methods break down — the gfoRmula package implements the full longitudinal parametric g-formula. We use the glm + marginaleffects route here because it makes every step transparent, but stdReg2 is an excellent production tool.

24.5 Putting It Together: Naive vs IPW vs G-Computation

The point of the chapter is best seen in one side-by-side comparison. The chunk below estimates the drug’s effect three ways on identical data: a naive regression that ignores confounding, IPW, and g-computation.

  • R
  • Python
Code 
library(WeightIt)
library(marginaleffects)

# --- One confounded cohort, known true effect (protective) ---
set.seed(2024)
n <- 3000
age      <- rnorm(n, 65, 10)
egfr     <- rnorm(n, 60, 15)
comorbid <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.4)
treat    <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-1 + 0.03*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60) + 0.5*comorbid))
death    <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-1 + 0.04*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60) +
                                  0.6*comorbid - 0.7*treat))
dat <- data.frame(age, egfr, comorbid, treat, death)

# (a) NAIVE: ignore confounders entirely, compare raw mortality
naive <- glm(death ~ treat, data = dat, family = binomial)
naive_rd <- avg_comparisons(naive, variables = "treat")

# (b) IPW: stabilised weights + weighted outcome model
W   <- weightit(treat ~ age + egfr + comorbid, data = dat,
                method = "glm", estimand = "ATE", stabilize = TRUE)
ipw_rd <- avg_comparisons(glm_weightit(death ~ treat, data = dat, weightit = W,
                                       family = binomial), variables = "treat")

# (c) G-COMPUTATION: outcome model + standardisation
gcomp_rd <- avg_comparisons(glm(death ~ treat * (age + egfr + comorbid),
                                data = dat, family = binomial),
                            variables = "treat")

naive_rd; ipw_rd; gcomp_rd

What the code shows. Three estimates of the same risk difference on the same data. The naive model regresses death on treatment alone, so it absorbs all the confounding: because sicker patients were preferentially treated, it will report a risk difference near zero or even positive — making a genuinely protective drug look useless or harmful. The IPW and g-computation estimates each adjust for age, egfr, and comorbid (one through weighting, one through outcome modelling) and should both land close to the true protective effect, i.e. a clearly negative risk difference. Seeing the naive estimate disagree with two methods that agree with each other is the whole lesson: confounding is not a rounding error, and the route you use to adjust matters far less than adjusting at all.

Code 
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

# --- One confounded cohort, known true effect (protective) ---
rng = np.random.default_rng(2024)
n = 3000
age      = rng.normal(65, 10, n)
egfr     = rng.normal(60, 15, n)
comorbid = rng.binomial(1, 0.4, n)
treat    = rng.binomial(1, 1/(1+np.exp(-(-1 + 0.03*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60) + 0.5*comorbid))))
death    = rng.binomial(1, 1/(1+np.exp(-(-1 + 0.04*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60)
                                          + 0.6*comorbid - 0.7*treat))))
df = pd.DataFrame(dict(age=age, egfr=egfr, comorbid=comorbid, treat=treat, death=death))

# (a) NAIVE: raw difference in mortality
naive = smf.glm("death ~ treat", data=df, family=sm.families.Binomial()).fit(disp=0)
naive_rd = naive.predict(df.assign(treat=1)).mean() - naive.predict(df.assign(treat=0)).mean()

# (b) IPW: stabilised weights + weighted outcome model
ps = smf.logit("treat ~ age + egfr + comorbid", data=df).fit(disp=0).predict(df)
p = df.treat.mean()
sw = np.where(df.treat == 1, p/ps, (1-p)/(1-ps))
mw = smf.glm("death ~ treat", data=df, family=sm.families.Binomial(),
             var_weights=sw).fit()
ipw_rd = mw.predict(df.assign(treat=1)).mean() - mw.predict(df.assign(treat=0)).mean()

# (c) G-COMPUTATION
gm = smf.glm("death ~ treat * (age + egfr + comorbid)", data=df,
             family=sm.families.Binomial()).fit(disp=0)
gcomp_rd = gm.predict(df.assign(treat=1)).mean() - gm.predict(df.assign(treat=0)).mean()

print(f"Naive risk difference:        {naive_rd:+.4f}")
print(f"IPW risk difference:          {ipw_rd:+.4f}")
print(f"G-computation risk difference:{gcomp_rd:+.4f}")

What the code shows. The Python version of the same three-way comparison, written out so each estimator is fully visible. The naive risk difference ignores confounding and will be biased toward (or past) zero; the IPW and g-computation risk differences adjust for the three confounders and should both be clearly negative, recovering the protective effect we built in. The take-home is identical to the R version: naive disagrees, the two principled methods agree.

24.6 IPW vs G-Computation, and a Word on “Best of Both”

The two methods are complementary, and choosing between them comes down to which model you trust more.

Inverse probability weighting G-computation
What you model The treatment (propensity score) The outcome
Main risk A wrong treatment model, or extreme weights (positivity) A wrong outcome model (functional form, interactions)
Strengths Natural for time-varying treatment (marginal structural models); transparent pseudo-population Uses all data efficiently; no weight explosions; easy to add interactions
Confidence interval Robust/sandwich or bootstrap Bootstrap

You do not have to pick. Doubly robust estimators — augmented IPW (AIPW) and targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE), which we previewed last chapter — combine both a treatment model and an outcome model and stay valid as long as at least one of the two is correct. They are the modern “best of both worlds” default, and once you understand IPW and g-computation you already understand their two ingredients. In R they live in packages such as AIPW and tmle; the simplest mental model is “g-computation, then nudge the prediction using the weights to correct any outcome-model mistakes.”

WarningThe assumption that no method can rescue

Every estimator in this chapter — naive, IPW, g-computation, even doubly robust — relies on no unmeasured confounding (the exchangeability assumption from last chapter). If illness severity has a component you did not measure, all of them are biased in the same direction. The g-methods give you a correct answer to the question you asked of the data you have; they cannot conjure information you never collected. This is why the E-value sensitivity analysis from last chapter belongs in every causal report.

TipKey Takeaways
  • A treatment-coefficient from a single naive regression is not generally a causal effect. The g-methods compute the effect the way its definition demands.
  • IPW models who gets treated and re-weights the sample; g-computation models the outcome and standardises. Both target the average treatment effect and both recover it when confounding is fully measured.
  • Always check balance and positivity for IPW, and always bootstrap g-computation’s confidence interval.
  • When the two methods agree, that agreement is itself reassuring; when they disagree, suspect a misspecified model or shaky positivity.
  • Doubly robust estimators (AIPW, TMLE) combine both ingredients and forgive a single modelling mistake — a sensible default once you understand the two foundations.

# Estimating Treatment Effects: Inverse Probability Weighting and G-Computation {#sec-g-methods}

Imagine you are handed a hospital database and a single, deceptively simple question: does this blood-pressure drug actually *prevent* deaths, or does it only *look* protective because the doctors who prescribe it tend to pick their healthier patients? In the previous chapter we met this problem --- confounding by indication --- and we learned to draw it out with a directed acyclic graph (DAG), to estimate a propensity score, and to balance groups by matching. This chapter takes the next step: once the groups are balanced, how do we actually *compute* the treatment effect, with an honest confidence interval, in a way that recovers the truth? Two methods do almost all the heavy lifting in modern causal analysis, and both come from the work of the epidemiologist James Robins. They are called the **g-methods** ("g" for *generalised*). This chapter is a slow, hands-on tour of the two foundational ones: **inverse probability weighting** and **g-computation**.

## Introduction

In the last chapter we built the conceptual scaffolding. We said that every patient has two *potential outcomes* --- what would happen if treated, and what would happen if untreated --- and that the causal effect is the contrast between them. We also said that the central obstacle is confounding: the treated and untreated patients differ systematically, so a raw comparison is biased. We introduced the propensity score (a patient's probability of being treated given their characteristics) and used it to match patients and, briefly, to weight them.

This chapter answers the practical question that follows: **given a balanced or balanceable dataset, how do I produce a single, defensible number for the average treatment effect?** There are two foundational answers, and it is worth knowing both because they make their assumptions in different places:

- **Inverse probability weighting (IPW)** models *who gets treated*. It builds a re-weighted "pseudo-population" in which treatment is no longer tied to the confounders, then compares outcomes in that pseudo-population.
- **G-computation** (also called the *parametric g-formula* or *standardisation*) models *what the outcome would be*. It fits an outcome model, then uses it to predict every patient's outcome twice --- once as if treated, once as if untreated --- and contrasts the averages.

Both target the same quantity, the average treatment effect (ATE) we defined last chapter, and under the same core assumption (no unmeasured confounding) both recover it. We will build each from first principles, run them on simulated data where we *know* the right answer, and show that a naive regression gets it wrong while the g-methods get it right.

::: {.callout-important}
## Why two methods instead of one?
A naive analyst fits one regression and reads off a coefficient. The trouble is that a regression coefficient for treatment is only the causal effect under restrictive conditions (no interactions, correct functional form, a collapsible effect measure). The g-methods sidestep these traps by computing the effect *the way the definition demands* --- as a contrast of average potential outcomes --- rather than trusting a single coefficient to mean what we hope it means. And because IPW and g-computation lean on different models, agreement between them is itself reassuring evidence.
:::

## The Running Clinical Example

Throughout this chapter we use one scenario. We have observational data on patients started (or not) on a hypothetical drug, and we want its effect on one-year mortality. The catch, as always, is **confounding by indication**: sicker patients --- older, with worse kidney function, more comorbidity --- are *both* more likely to be treated *and* more likely to die regardless of treatment. A raw comparison will therefore make the drug look useless or even harmful, even if it genuinely saves lives. Our job is to undo that distortion.

## Inverse Probability Weighting

### The idea in plain language

Recall the propensity score from last chapter: each patient's probability of receiving the treatment they were eligible for, given their measured characteristics [@haukoos2015propensity]. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (often shortened to IPW or IPTW) uses that score to **re-weight** the sample.

Here is the intuition, set out accessibly for clinical readers by @mansournia2016ipw and @chesnaye2022ipw. Suppose a very sick patient --- the kind clinicians almost always treat --- nevertheless went *untreated*. That patient is rare and precious: they are one of the few untreated people who *look like* the treated group, so they carry a lot of information about what happens to treated-type patients when they go without the drug. IPW gives such a patient a large weight. Conversely, an untreated patient who looked very unlikely to be treated anyway is "over-represented" among controls, so IPW shrinks their weight. Do this for everyone and you build a **pseudo-population** in which being treated is statistically unrelated to the confounders --- exactly the situation a randomised trial creates by design.

The weight for each patient is the inverse of the probability of the treatment they actually received:

$$
w_i = \frac{A_i}{e(L_i)} + \frac{1 - A_i}{1 - e(L_i)}
$$

where $A_i$ is 1 if treated and 0 if not, and $e(L_i)$ is the propensity score. A treated patient with propensity score 0.2 gets weight $1/0.2 = 5$; an untreated patient with propensity score 0.9 (so probability of being untreated $= 0.1$) gets weight $1/0.1 = 10$. These weights are larger precisely for the informative, "surprising" patients.

### Stabilised weights

The raw weights above can occasionally become enormous --- if a treated patient had a propensity score of 0.01, their weight is 100, and a single patient can then dominate the analysis and inflate the variance. **Stabilised weights** tame this by replacing the "1" in the numerator with the overall probability of receiving that treatment:

$$
sw_i = \frac{A_i \cdot P(A=1)}{e(L_i)} + \frac{(1 - A_i) \cdot P(A=0)}{1 - e(L_i)}
$$

The estimate is unchanged on average, but the weights cluster sensibly around 1 and the confidence intervals are narrower and more honest. Stabilised weights are the default recommendation and what modern packages produce automatically.

::: {.callout-note}
## Marginal structural models in one line
When you fit an outcome model (say, a regression of mortality on treatment) *using these weights*, the model you have fitted is called a **marginal structural model (MSM)**. "Marginal" because it describes the whole population (not conditional on confounders), and "structural" because its coefficients have a causal interpretation. MSMs are the natural home of IPW and the standard tool when a treatment changes over time --- a setting where ordinary regression genuinely cannot give the right answer.
:::

### Checking the weights worked: balance and positivity

Weighting is only trustworthy if it actually balanced the confounders, and only valid if every kind of patient could in principle have received either treatment. So two checks are mandatory:

1. **Covariate balance.** After weighting, the treated and control groups should have a near-identical mix of confounders. We measure this with the *standardised mean difference* (SMD) --- the difference in a covariate's mean between groups, divided by its standard deviation --- and want every value comfortably below 0.1.
2. **Positivity.** Recall from last chapter: every patient must have a non-zero chance of either treatment. In weighting terms, a propensity score near 0 or 1 produces an explosive weight. So we inspect the largest weights; a maximum weight above roughly 10--20 is a warning sign that positivity is shaky and the estimate may be unstable.

```{r}
#| eval: false

library(WeightIt)        # estimate balancing weights
library(cobalt)          # check covariate balance
library(marginaleffects) # turn the weighted model into an effect

# --- Simulate a confounded cohort with a KNOWN true effect ---
set.seed(2024)
n <- 3000
age       <- rnorm(n, 65, 10)
egfr      <- rnorm(n, 60, 15)               # kidney function
comorbid  <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.4)              # any major comorbidity

# Sicker patients (older, worse kidneys, comorbid) are MORE likely treated
lp_treat  <- -1 + 0.03 * (age - 65) - 0.03 * (egfr - 60) + 0.5 * comorbid
ps_true   <- plogis(lp_treat)
treat     <- rbinom(n, 1, ps_true)

# TRUE causal effect of treatment on mortality: log-odds -0.7 (protective)
lp_death  <- -1 + 0.04 * (age - 65) - 0.03 * (egfr - 60) + 0.6 * comorbid - 0.7 * treat
death     <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(lp_death))

dat <- data.frame(age, egfr, comorbid, treat, death)

# --- Step 1: estimate stabilised weights for the ATE ---
W <- weightit(treat ~ age + egfr + comorbid,
              data = dat, method = "glm", estimand = "ATE",
              stabilize = TRUE)

# --- Step 2: check balance and the size of the weights ---
bal.tab(W, thresholds = c(m = 0.1))   # SMDs after weighting; want < 0.1
summary(W)                            # inspect the largest weights (positivity)

# --- Step 3: fit the weighted outcome model (a marginal structural model) ---
msm <- glm_weightit(death ~ treat, data = dat, weightit = W,
                    family = binomial)

# --- Step 4: read off the causal effect on the risk-difference scale ---
avg_comparisons(msm, variables = "treat")
```

**What the code shows.** We first manufacture a cohort where treatment is deliberately confounded and the *true* protective effect is built in (the `- 0.7 * treat` term in the mortality equation). `weightit()` estimates the propensity score and converts it into stabilised ATE weights in one call. `bal.tab()` then prints a table of standardised mean differences for `age`, `egfr`, and `comorbid` *after* weighting --- you want every number under 0.1, confirming the pseudo-population is balanced. `summary(W)` reports the range of weights; a sane maximum (well under 20 here) means positivity is not violated. Finally `glm_weightit()` fits the weighted outcome model --- this is the marginal structural model --- and `avg_comparisons()` converts it into a clean *risk difference*: the average change in the probability of death caused by the drug, with a confidence interval that correctly accounts for the weighting. Because we hard-wired a protective effect, you should see a negative risk difference (fewer deaths under treatment), unlike the naive analysis we run later.

### Python

```{python}
#| label: ipw-py
#| eval: false

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

# --- Simulate a confounded cohort with a KNOWN true effect ---
rng = np.random.default_rng(2024)
n = 3000
age      = rng.normal(65, 10, n)
egfr     = rng.normal(60, 15, n)
comorbid = rng.binomial(1, 0.4, n)

lp_treat = -1 + 0.03*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60) + 0.5*comorbid
ps_true  = 1/(1+np.exp(-lp_treat))
treat    = rng.binomial(1, ps_true)

# TRUE causal effect: log-odds -0.7 (protective)
lp_death = -1 + 0.04*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60) + 0.6*comorbid - 0.7*treat
death    = rng.binomial(1, 1/(1+np.exp(-lp_death)))

df = pd.DataFrame(dict(age=age, egfr=egfr, comorbid=comorbid,
                       treat=treat, death=death))

# --- Step 1: estimate the propensity score ---
ps_model = smf.logit("treat ~ age + egfr + comorbid", data=df).fit(disp=0)
df["ps"] = ps_model.predict(df)

# --- Step 2: stabilised weights ---
p_treat = df["treat"].mean()
df["sw"] = np.where(df["treat"] == 1,
                    p_treat / df["ps"],
                    (1 - p_treat) / (1 - df["ps"]))

# --- Step 3: check balance (weighted standardised mean differences) ---
def wsmd(v):
    t, c = df[df.treat == 1], df[df.treat == 0]
    mt = np.average(t[v], weights=t.sw)
    mc = np.average(c[v], weights=c.sw)
    sd = np.sqrt((t[v].var() + c[v].var()) / 2)
    return (mt - mc) / sd

print("Weighted SMDs (want |value| < 0.1):")
for v in ["age", "egfr", "comorbid"]:
    print(f"  {v}: {wsmd(v):+.3f}")
print("Max weight (positivity check):", df["sw"].max().round(2))

# --- Step 4: weighted outcome model on the risk-difference scale ---
msm = smf.glm("death ~ treat", data=df, family=sm.families.Binomial(),
              var_weights=df["sw"]).fit()
# Convert to a marginal risk difference by predicting under treat=0/1
d1 = df.assign(treat=1); d0 = df.assign(treat=0)
rd = (msm.predict(d1) - msm.predict(d0)).mean()
print(f"\nIPW risk difference (treat - control): {rd:+.4f}")
```

**What the code shows.** This is the same workflow written from scratch so every step is visible. We simulate the identical confounded cohort, fit a logistic propensity-score model with `statsmodels`, and build stabilised weights by hand. The `wsmd` helper prints the weighted standardised mean difference for each confounder --- all should be near zero (under 0.1) if weighting balanced the groups --- and we print the maximum weight as a positivity check. We then fit the weighted outcome model and, rather than reading a single coefficient, we predict each patient's death probability twice (everyone treated, then everyone untreated) and average the difference. That average is the *risk difference*, directly comparable to the R output, and should be negative because we built in a protective effect.

:::

## G-Computation

### The idea in plain language

IPW models the *treatment*. **G-computation** instead models the *outcome*, and it follows the definition of the average treatment effect almost literally.

Recall the definition: the ATE is the average outcome if *everyone* were treated minus the average outcome if *everyone* were untreated. We never observe both worlds for any patient --- but we can *predict* them. The recipe has three steps:

1. **Fit one outcome model** that includes treatment *and* all the confounders (and, ideally, their interactions). This model learns how outcome depends jointly on treatment and patient characteristics.
2. **Predict twice for every patient.** First set everyone's treatment to "treated" (leaving their real confounders untouched) and predict each patient's outcome. Then reset everyone to "untreated" and predict again. Each patient now has two predicted outcomes --- their estimated potential outcomes.
3. **Average and contrast.** Average the "all treated" predictions, average the "all untreated" predictions, and take the difference. That difference *is* the average treatment effect.

This procedure is also called **standardisation**, because it standardises both arms to the *same* confounder distribution (the whole sample's). It is the oldest idea in the chapter --- Robins introduced the g-formula in 1986 --- and it remains one of the most reliable.

### Confidence intervals: use the bootstrap

Because g-computation predicts, averages, and contrasts, the uncertainty in the final number does not come out of the outcome model directly. The standard solution is the **bootstrap**: resample the patients with replacement many times, rerun the whole predict-average-contrast procedure on each resample, and use the spread of the resulting estimates as the confidence interval. Modern tooling automates this --- in R, `marginaleffects::inferences()` wraps the bootstrap around the whole calculation.

### G-computation in practice

```{r}

#| eval: false

library(marginaleffects)  # avg_comparisons() + inferences() bootstrap

# --- Simulate the SAME confounded cohort with a known true effect ---
set.seed(2024)
n <- 3000
age       <- rnorm(n, 65, 10)
egfr      <- rnorm(n, 60, 15)
comorbid  <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.4)
ps_true   <- plogis(-1 + 0.03*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60) + 0.5*comorbid)
treat     <- rbinom(n, 1, ps_true)
death     <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-1 + 0.04*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60) +
                                   0.6*comorbid - 0.7*treat))
dat <- data.frame(age, egfr, comorbid, treat, death)

# --- Step 1: fit ONE outcome model with treatment + confounders ---
#     Include treatment-confounder interactions so effects can vary by patient
out_model <- glm(death ~ treat * (age + egfr + comorbid),
                 data = dat, family = binomial)

# --- Steps 2-4: predict under treat=1 and treat=0, average, contrast ---
#     avg_comparisons does the standardisation; inferences() bootstraps the CI
avg_comparisons(out_model, variables = "treat") |>
  inferences(method = "boot", R = 500)
```

**What the code shows.** We rebuild the identical confounded cohort (this chunk is fully self-contained). We then fit a *single* logistic outcome model, deliberately including `treat * (age + egfr + comorbid)` so the drug's effect is allowed to differ across patient types --- a flexibility plain regression coefficients cannot capture. `avg_comparisons(..., variables = "treat")` carries out the g-computation automatically: it predicts every patient's death probability with `treat` set to 1, again with it set to 0, averages each, and reports the difference as a risk difference. Piping into `inferences(method = "boot", R = 500)` wraps the whole procedure in a 500-replicate bootstrap so the confidence interval is honest. The estimate should be negative and close to the IPW estimate above --- two different models, agreeing on the truth.

### Python

```{python}
#| label: gcomp-py
#| eval: false

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

# --- Simulate the SAME confounded cohort ---
rng = np.random.default_rng(2024)
n = 3000
age      = rng.normal(65, 10, n)
egfr     = rng.normal(60, 15, n)
comorbid = rng.binomial(1, 0.4, n)
ps_true  = 1/(1+np.exp(-(-1 + 0.03*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60) + 0.5*comorbid)))
treat    = rng.binomial(1, ps_true)
death    = rng.binomial(1, 1/(1+np.exp(-(-1 + 0.04*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60)
                                          + 0.6*comorbid - 0.7*treat))))
df = pd.DataFrame(dict(age=age, egfr=egfr, comorbid=comorbid,
                       treat=treat, death=death))

# --- The g-computation procedure as a reusable function ---
def gcomp(data):
    m = smf.glm("death ~ treat * (age + egfr + comorbid)",
                data=data, family=sm.families.Binomial()).fit(disp=0)
    d1 = data.assign(treat=1)   # everyone treated
    d0 = data.assign(treat=0)   # everyone untreated
    return m.predict(d1).mean() - m.predict(d0).mean()

point = gcomp(df)

# --- Bootstrap the confidence interval ---
rng_boot = np.random.default_rng(1)
boot = np.array([
    gcomp(df.sample(len(df), replace=True, random_state=int(rng_boot.integers(1e9))))
    for _ in range(500)
])
lo, hi = np.percentile(boot, [2.5, 97.5])
print(f"G-computation risk difference: {point:+.4f}  (95% CI {lo:+.4f}, {hi:+.4f})")
```

**What the code shows.** We wrap the predict-average-contrast recipe in a function, `gcomp`, that fits the interaction outcome model, predicts every patient's death probability under "everyone treated" and "everyone untreated", and returns the average difference. Calling it once on the real data gives the point estimate. We then call it 500 more times on bootstrap resamples (sampling patients with replacement) and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of those estimates as a 95 percent confidence interval. The printed risk difference should be negative and close to the R g-computation result and the IPW result --- demonstrating that, when the assumptions hold, the route you take to the answer matters less than getting the confounding adjustment right.

:::

```{r}
age      <- rnorm(n, 65, 10)
egfr     <- rnorm(n, 60, 15)
comorbid <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.4)
treat    <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-1 + 0.03*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60) + 0.5*comorbid))
death    <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-1 + 0.04*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60) +
                                  0.6*comorbid - 0.7*treat))
dat <- data.frame(age, egfr, comorbid, treat, death)

# (a) NAIVE: ignore confounders entirely, compare raw mortality
naive <- glm(death ~ treat, data = dat, family = binomial)
naive_rd <- avg_comparisons(naive, variables = "treat")

# (b) IPW: stabilised weights + weighted outcome model
W   <- weightit(treat ~ age + egfr + comorbid, data = dat,
                method = "glm", estimand = "ATE", stabilize = TRUE)
ipw_rd <- avg_comparisons(glm_weightit(death ~ treat, data = dat, weightit = W,
                                       family = binomial), variables = "treat")

# (c) G-COMPUTATION: outcome model + standardisation
gcomp_rd <- avg_comparisons(glm(death ~ treat * (age + egfr + comorbid),
                                data = dat, family = binomial),
                            variables = "treat")

naive_rd; ipw_rd; gcomp_rd
```

**What the code shows.** Three estimates of the same risk difference on the same data. The **naive** model regresses death on treatment alone, so it absorbs all the confounding: because sicker patients were preferentially treated, it will report a risk difference near zero or even positive --- making a genuinely protective drug look useless or harmful. The **IPW** and **g-computation** estimates each adjust for `age`, `egfr`, and `comorbid` (one through weighting, one through outcome modelling) and should both land close to the true protective effect, i.e. a clearly negative risk difference. Seeing the naive estimate disagree with two methods that agree with each other is the whole lesson: confounding is not a rounding error, and the route you use to adjust matters far less than adjusting at all.

### Python

```{python}
#| label: compare-py
#| eval: false

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

# --- One confounded cohort, known true effect (protective) ---
rng = np.random.default_rng(2024)
n = 3000
age      = rng.normal(65, 10, n)
egfr     = rng.normal(60, 15, n)
comorbid = rng.binomial(1, 0.4, n)
treat    = rng.binomial(1, 1/(1+np.exp(-(-1 + 0.03*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60) + 0.5*comorbid))))
death    = rng.binomial(1, 1/(1+np.exp(-(-1 + 0.04*(age-65) - 0.03*(egfr-60)
                                          + 0.6*comorbid - 0.7*treat))))
df = pd.DataFrame(dict(age=age, egfr=egfr, comorbid=comorbid, treat=treat, death=death))

# (a) NAIVE: raw difference in mortality
naive = smf.glm("death ~ treat", data=df, family=sm.families.Binomial()).fit(disp=0)
naive_rd = naive.predict(df.assign(treat=1)).mean() - naive.predict(df.assign(treat=0)).mean()

# (b) IPW: stabilised weights + weighted outcome model
ps = smf.logit("treat ~ age + egfr + comorbid", data=df).fit(disp=0).predict(df)
p = df.treat.mean()
sw = np.where(df.treat == 1, p/ps, (1-p)/(1-ps))
mw = smf.glm("death ~ treat", data=df, family=sm.families.Binomial(),
             var_weights=sw).fit()
ipw_rd = mw.predict(df.assign(treat=1)).mean() - mw.predict(df.assign(treat=0)).mean()

# (c) G-COMPUTATION
gm = smf.glm("death ~ treat * (age + egfr + comorbid)", data=df,
             family=sm.families.Binomial()).fit(disp=0)
gcomp_rd = gm.predict(df.assign(treat=1)).mean() - gm.predict(df.assign(treat=0)).mean()

print(f"Naive risk difference:        {naive_rd:+.4f}")
print(f"IPW risk difference:          {ipw_rd:+.4f}")
print(f"G-computation risk difference:{gcomp_rd:+.4f}")
```

**What the code shows.** The Python version of the same three-way comparison, written out so each estimator is fully visible. The naive risk difference ignores confounding and will be biased toward (or past) zero; the IPW and g-computation risk differences adjust for the three confounders and should both be clearly negative, recovering the protective effect we built in. The take-home is identical to the R version: naive disagrees, the two principled methods agree.

:::

## IPW vs G-Computation, and a Word on "Best of Both"

The two methods are complementary, and choosing between them comes down to which model you trust more.

| | Inverse probability weighting | G-computation |
|---|---|---|
| What you model | The **treatment** (propensity score) | The **outcome** |
| Main risk | A wrong treatment model, or extreme weights (positivity) | A wrong outcome model (functional form, interactions) |
| Strengths | Natural for time-varying treatment (marginal structural models); transparent pseudo-population | Uses all data efficiently; no weight explosions; easy to add interactions |
| Confidence interval | Robust/sandwich or bootstrap | Bootstrap |

You do not have to pick. **Doubly robust** estimators --- augmented IPW (AIPW) and targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE), which we previewed last chapter --- combine *both* a treatment model and an outcome model and stay valid as long as *at least one* of the two is correct. They are the modern "best of both worlds" default, and once you understand IPW and g-computation you already understand their two ingredients. In R they live in packages such as `AIPW` and `tmle`; the simplest mental model is "g-computation, then nudge the prediction using the weights to correct any outcome-model mistakes."

::: {.callout-warning}
## The assumption that no method can rescue
Every estimator in this chapter --- naive, IPW, g-computation, even doubly robust --- relies on **no unmeasured confounding** (the exchangeability assumption from last chapter). If illness severity has a component you did not measure, all of them are biased in the same direction. The g-methods give you a correct answer *to the question you asked of the data you have*; they cannot conjure information you never collected. This is why the E-value sensitivity analysis from last chapter belongs in every causal report.
:::

## Summary

| Concept | Key point |
|---|---|
| G-methods | Robins' family of methods for estimating causal effects under confounding |
| Inverse probability weighting | Re-weight by 1/P(received treatment) to build an unconfounded pseudo-population |
| Stabilised weights | Multiply by the marginal treatment probability to tame extreme weights |
| Marginal structural model | An outcome model fitted with IP weights; coefficients are causal |
| Positivity check | Inspect the largest weights; a maximum above ~20 is a red flag |
| G-computation | Fit one outcome model, predict everyone treated then untreated, average and contrast |
| Standardisation | Another name for g-computation; both arms standardised to the same confounder mix |
| Bootstrap | The standard way to get confidence intervals for g-computation |
| Doubly robust (AIPW/TMLE) | Combine treatment and outcome models; valid if either is correct |

::: {.callout-tip}
## Key Takeaways
- A treatment-coefficient from a single naive regression is **not** generally a causal effect. The g-methods compute the effect the way its definition demands.
- **IPW** models *who gets treated* and re-weights the sample; **g-computation** models *the outcome* and standardises. Both target the average treatment effect and both recover it when confounding is fully measured.
- Always **check balance and positivity** for IPW, and always **bootstrap** g-computation's confidence interval.
- When the two methods agree, that agreement is itself reassuring; when they disagree, suspect a misspecified model or shaky positivity.
- **Doubly robust** estimators (AIPW, TMLE) combine both ingredients and forgive a single modelling mistake --- a sensible default once you understand the two foundations.
- No method overcomes **unmeasured confounding**. Pair every causal estimate with a sensitivity analysis such as the E-value.
:::
