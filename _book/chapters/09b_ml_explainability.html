A random forest or an XGBoost model will often out-predict your trusty logistic regression. That is the good news. The bad news is that when a colleague, a patient, or a regulator asks “why did the model say this patient is high risk?”, you may have no answer. The model is a black box: data goes in, a number comes out, and the hundreds of trees that produced it are far too tangled to read by eye. This chapter is about prising that box open — not by abandoning the accurate model, but by using a separate set of tools that interrogate it after it has been trained and tell us, in human terms, what it learned and why it made a particular prediction.

17.1 Introduction

In the previous chapters you built tree ensembles that capture non-linear effects and interactions automatically, and you learned how to measure whether their predictions are any good. But a model that predicts well is not the same as a model you can trust. Consider why a clinician should care about explaining a model, not just scoring it:

  • Trust and adoption. A clinician will not act on a risk score they cannot reconcile with their own reasoning. If the model flags a patient as high risk, the natural next question is “on what grounds?” — and “the algorithm said so” is not an acceptable answer at the bedside. The case that explainability is a precondition for clinicians to accept AI tools, and the practical forms it can take, is set out for a medical audience by Reddy (Reddy 2022).
  • Catching the model relying on the wrong thing. Models latch onto whatever predicts the outcome in the training data, even when it is clinically nonsensical. A famous class of failures: a model appears to predict pneumonia severity brilliantly, but it has really learned to read which hospital scanner took the X-ray (sicker patients went to a particular machine), or it keys on a code that is a proxy for access to care rather than for the disease itself. The model is right for the wrong reason, and it will fail the moment it meets a new hospital.
  • Fairness. Explanations help reveal whether a model leans on variables (or proxies for them) that would make it perform unequally across groups of patients.
  • Regulation and reporting. The TRIPOD+AI reporting guideline (Collins et al. 2024), published in 2024, now expects authors of clinical prediction models to describe how the model can be interpreted. Explainability has moved from a nice-to-have to an expectation.
NoteWhy a clinician should care

An accurate prediction you cannot explain is a clinical liability, not an asset. The first time a black-box model confidently makes an indefensible recommendation — and you cannot say why — trust in the whole tool collapses. Explainability is how you find the model’s blind spots before a patient does.

A crucial distinction runs through this entire chapter, so we set it out at the start.

  • Global explanations answer: “What drives the model overall, across all patients?” These tell you which variables the model relies on most and in which direction, summarising the model’s general behaviour.
  • Local explanations answer: “Why did the model give this patient this prediction?” These break a single prediction down into the contribution of each of that patient’s characteristics.

Both are needed. A global view tells you the model broadly behaves sensibly; a local view lets you justify the number in front of an individual patient. Figure 17.1 lays out the methods we will cover and where each one sits.

One warning before we start, which we will return to in force at the end: every method in this chapter explains the associations the model learned, not the biology of disease. An explanation tells you how the model is behaving. It does not tell you that the model is correct, and it certainly does not establish cause and effect. Keep that firmly in mind — a tidy, plausible-looking explanation is one of the most seductive ways to be misled.

Throughout, we will use a single running scenario: a model predicting the risk of an unplanned hospital readmission within 30 days of discharge, built from routine variables (age, length of stay, number of comorbidities, prior admissions, a few discharge labs). Each code chunk re-creates its own data and re-fits its own model, so you can run any one of them on its own.

17.2 Permutation Feature Importance (Global)

The simplest global question is: which variables does the model actually need? Permutation feature importance answers it with a beautifully direct idea. Take your trained model and a dataset where you know the true outcomes. Now pick one variable — say, prior admissions — and shuffle its values randomly between patients, so that each patient now has someone else’s number of prior admissions. This deliberately destroys any real relationship between that variable and the outcome, while leaving every other variable untouched. Re-score the model on this scrambled data. If performance (say, the area under the ROC curve, or AUC) drops a lot, the model was leaning heavily on that variable — it mattered. If performance barely changes, the model was not really using it.

The word “permutation” simply means shuffling. We repeat the shuffle a few times for each variable to average out the randomness, and we do this for every variable in turn. The result is a ranked list: the variables whose shuffling hurts the model most are the most important to it.

This is the recommended general-purpose importance measure because it asks the right question — how much does the model’s accuracy depend on this variable? — and it works for any model, not just trees. (It is more honest than the built-in “impurity” importance you met in the trees chapter, which can be biased toward variables with many distinct values.)

import pandas as pd
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.inspection import permutation_importance
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- Re-create the readmission data inside this chunk ---
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1500
X = pd.DataFrame({
    "age":               rng.normal(68, 12, n),
    "length_of_stay":    rng.poisson(5, n) + 1,
    "num_comorbidities": rng.poisson(3, n),
    "prior_admissions":  rng.poisson(1, n),
    "discharge_hb":      rng.normal(11, 2, n),
    "discharge_creat":   rng.lognormal(0.2, 0.5, n),
})
lin = (-3 + 0.45 * X["prior_admissions"] + 0.20 * X["num_comorbidities"]
       + 0.015 * (X["age"] - 68) - 0.05 * X["discharge_hb"])
y = rng.binomial(1, 1 / (1 + np.exp(-lin)))

# --- Fit a random forest ---
rf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=500, random_state=42, n_jobs=-1)
rf.fit(X, y)

# --- Permutation importance (shuffle each column, watch AUC fall) ---
result = permutation_importance(rf, X, y, scoring="roc_auc",
                                n_repeats=10, random_state=1)
order = result.importances_mean.argsort()
plt.barh(X.columns[order], result.importances_mean[order], color="#2E86AB")
plt.xlabel("Mean drop in AUC when the variable is shuffled")
plt.title("Permutation feature importance")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

What the output shows. You will see a horizontal bar chart with one bar per variable, sorted from most to least important. The length of each bar is how much the model’s AUC fell when that variable was shuffled. Because we built the data so that prior admissions and number of comorbidities drive the true risk, those two bars should be the longest, with age contributing a little and the discharge labs contributing almost nothing. Read it like this: the model genuinely relies on prior admissions and comorbidity count, exactly the variables a clinician would expect to matter for readmission — which is reassuring. If, instead, the longest bar had been something clinically irrelevant (a patient ID, an admission timestamp, the name of the discharging ward), that would be a red flag that the model has latched onto a quirk of how the data were collected rather than the disease process. Permutation importance is your first, cheapest check that the model is paying attention to the right things.

17.3 Partial Dependence Plots (Global)

Knowing that age matters does not tell you how it matters — does risk climb steadily with age, jump only after 75, or even fall again in the very old? A partial dependence plot (PDP) answers the “how” by showing the average shape of the relationship the model learned between one variable and its prediction.

The idea, in plain terms: to see how the model’s prediction depends on age, take your whole dataset and set everybody’s age to 50, then ask the model for the average predicted risk. Now set everybody’s age to 51, average again. Repeat across the full range of ages. Plot the average predicted risk against age, and you have traced out the relationship the model uses, holding the mix of all other variables fixed at their real values. The line might be flat (age does not move the prediction), steadily rising (more age, more risk), or curved (risk accelerates past a threshold) — and crucially, you did not have to tell the model to look for any of those shapes; the PDP reveals whatever the model discovered on its own.

WarningWhen predictors are correlated, prefer ALE

The PDP has a known weakness. When you force everybody’s age to 90 while leaving their other variables untouched, you create impossible patients — a 90-year-old with the bloodwork of a 40-year-old — and the model is asked to predict for combinations it never saw. When predictors are strongly correlated (as clinical variables usually are), this can distort the curve.

Accumulated Local Effects (ALE) plots fix this. Instead of forcing unrealistic values on everyone, ALE looks at how the prediction changes over small, realistic windows of the variable and accumulates those local changes. The result reads almost exactly like a PDP — the prediction on the y-axis, the variable on the x-axis — but it stays honest in the presence of correlation. When in doubt with correlated clinical predictors, plot the ALE. In R, iml and DALEX both compute ALE; in Python, the PyALE package does.

What the output shows. Each PDP is a single line. The x-axis is the variable (here, prior admissions); the y-axis is the model’s average predicted readmission risk as that variable is swept across its range. For prior admissions you should see a line that climbs from left to right: patients with more prior admissions are assigned higher risk by the model, and the steepness tells you how strongly. The Python version also draws age, where you would expect a gentler upward slope. To read a PDP clinically, ask three things: Which way does it go? (does more of this variable mean more or less predicted risk?), Is it a straight line or a curve? (a sharp bend signals a threshold the model found — e.g. risk only takes off after a certain age), and Does the shape make clinical sense? A PDP that sloped the “wrong” way — say, predicted risk falling as comorbidities rise — would be a signal to investigate, because it contradicts clinical expectation and may reveal confounding or a data problem. Remember the caveat above: with correlated predictors, confirm the shape with an ALE plot before trusting it.

17.4 SHAP: Shapley Values (Local and Global)

SHAP is the modern standard for explaining tabular models, so we will spend the most time here. The name stands for SHapley Additive exPlanations (Lundberg and Lee 2017), and its core idea is borrowed from cooperative game theory.

The intuition. Imagine a prediction is a “payout” that a team of players earned together, where the players are the patient’s variables (age, prior admissions, and so on). We want to fairly divide the credit for the prediction among the variables: how much did this patient’s age push the predicted risk up or down, compared with an average patient? In the 1950s, the economist Lloyd Shapley solved exactly this fair-division problem for games. His solution — the Shapley value — considers every possible order in which players could join the team and averages each player’s marginal contribution. It is the unique way of splitting the credit that satisfies a set of common-sense fairness rules (for instance, a variable that never changes the prediction gets zero credit). SHAP applies this idea to model predictions.

The payoff of all that theory is a property that makes SHAP uniquely useful: the SHAP values for one patient add up. Start from the model’s average prediction across everyone (the “baseline”), add each variable’s SHAP value for this patient, and you arrive exactly at this patient’s prediction. So SHAP gives you a complete, additive accounting of every individual prediction — nothing is left unexplained. Because each explanation is exact and additive, you can also average the magnitudes of SHAP values across many patients to get a trustworthy global importance ranking. This is why SHAP bridges the local and global worlds (recall Figure 17.1).

For tree ensembles there is a fast, exact algorithm called TreeSHAP, so computing SHAP for a random forest or XGBoost model on a clinical dataset is quick.

17.4.1 The SHAP summary (beeswarm) plot — global

The single most informative SHAP figure is the beeswarm summary plot, and it repays careful reading. Picture this: each variable gets one horizontal row, and the rows are stacked with the most important variable at the top. Within a row, there is one dot per patient. The dot’s horizontal position is that patient’s SHAP value for that variable — to the right means this variable pushed that patient’s predicted risk up, to the left means it pushed it down, and the further from the centre, the bigger the push. The dot’s colour encodes the variable’s value for that patient: typically red/high to blue/low.

So a single beeswarm row tells you both how important a variable is (how spread out the dots are) and which direction it acts (do high values, in red, sit on the right or the left?). For prior admissions, you would expect to see red dots (many prior admissions) clustered on the right (pushing risk up) and blue dots (few prior admissions) on the left (pushing risk down) — a clean, clinically sensible gradient. If a variable’s colours were scrambled with no clear left-right pattern, the model is using it in a complicated, non-monotonic way worth investigating.

17.4.2 SHAP dependence (scatter) plots — global

A SHAP dependence plot zooms into one variable. It plots, for every patient, the variable’s value on the x-axis against its SHAP value on the y-axis. This is like a PDP but built from the exact per-patient contributions, and it reveals the shape of the variable’s effect plus the spread caused by interactions with other variables (vertical scatter at a given x-value means the variable’s effect depends on something else).

17.4.3 Explaining one patient — local

For a single patient, the waterfall plot (or the equivalent force plot) is the clinician’s friend. It starts at the baseline (the average predicted risk) at the bottom and stacks one bar per variable, each pushing the running total up (red, risk-increasing) or down (blue, risk-decreasing), until it lands on this patient’s final predicted risk at the top. Read top to bottom, it is a plain sentence: “This patient’s risk is high mainly because they have four prior admissions (+12%) and a low discharge haemoglobin (+4%), partly offset by their relatively young age (−3%).” That is exactly the kind of justification you can put in a note or discuss with a patient.

model.fit(X, y)

# --- Compute SHAP values (TreeExplainer = fast exact TreeSHAP) ---
explainer = shap.Explainer(model, X)
shap_values = explainer(X)

# GLOBAL: beeswarm summary plot
shap.plots.beeswarm(shap_values, show=True)

# GLOBAL: dependence (scatter) plot for prior admissions
shap.plots.scatter(shap_values[:, "prior_admissions"], show=True)

# LOCAL: explain a single patient (row 0) with a waterfall plot
shap.plots.waterfall(shap_values[0], show=True)

What the output shows. Three figures, mapping onto the three SHAP uses.

  1. The beeswarm is your global summary. Variables are ranked top-to-bottom by overall importance, with prior admissions and num_comorbidities expected at the top. In each row, look at where the red (high-value) dots sit: for prior admissions they should lie to the right, confirming that more prior admissions push predicted risk up. This one plot replaces and enriches the permutation bar chart — it shows importance and direction at once.
  2. The dependence/scatter plot for prior admissions shows each patient as a dot, with the variable’s value across the bottom and its SHAP contribution up the side. An upward trend confirms the risk-increasing relationship; any vertical spread hints that the effect is modulated by other variables (an interaction).
  3. The waterfall explains one chosen patient. You will see the baseline risk at one end and the patient’s final predicted risk at the other, with coloured bars in between showing exactly which of their characteristics raised or lowered the prediction and by how much. This is the figure to show when someone asks “why is this patient flagged?”

Taken together, SHAP lets you audit the model globally and defend any single prediction locally, using one coherent, additive accounting. That combination is why it has become the default tool. For a concrete clinical example, Lundberg and colleagues used SHAP to explain a model that predicts intraoperative hypoxaemia in real time, and showed that surfacing the per-patient risk factors to anaesthetists improved their ability to anticipate events — a demonstration that local explanations can change behaviour at the bedside, not just on paper (Lundberg et al. 2018). A practical note: TreeSHAP is exact and fast for the tree models above; for other model types SHAP falls back to slower approximations (KernelExplainer in Python, kernelshap in R), so on large datasets you may explain a representative sample of patients rather than all of them.

17.5 LIME (Local)

LIME — Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (Ribeiro et al. 2016) — predates SHAP and is worth knowing because you will still see it in papers. Its idea is intuitive. To explain one patient’s prediction, LIME creates a cloud of slightly altered versions of that patient (nudging variables up and down), asks the black-box model to score all of them, and then fits a simple, transparent model — usually a small linear regression — to that local cloud. Because the simple model only has to be accurate near this one patient, its coefficients give a readable, local approximation: “in the neighbourhood of this patient, higher creatinine and more prior admissions are what drive the risk up.”

In practice LIME has largely been superseded by SHAP for tabular clinical data, because SHAP rests on firmer theory (the fairness guarantees of Shapley values) and its local explanations add up exactly to the prediction, whereas LIME’s depend on somewhat arbitrary choices (how big is the neighbourhood? how is “nearby” defined?) that can make explanations unstable. Reach for SHAP first; understand LIME so you can read older literature and appreciate the shared “explain locally with a simple surrogate” idea.

17.6 A Health Warning: What Explanations Do and Do Not Tell You

This is the most important section in the chapter. The tools above are powerful, and precisely because their output looks so authoritative, they are easy to over-trust. Ghassemi, Oakden-Rayner, and Beam make this case forcefully in The Lancet Digital Health, arguing that current explainability methods offer a “false hope” for patient-level decision support: an explanation can describe how the model behaves without ever assuring you that it reached its decision for a clinically valid reason, and they urge that rigorous internal and external validation — not explanation — is the more direct route to a trustworthy model (Ghassemi et al. 2021). Read that paper alongside this section.

ImportantExplanations show association, not causation — and not correctness
  • They describe the model, not the disease. A SHAP plot says “the model uses high creatinine to raise predicted risk.” It does not say “high creatinine causes readmission,” and it certainly does not license “lowering creatinine will prevent readmission.” The variable may be a proxy for sicker patients. Never read an importance ranking or a SHAP plot as a causal effect. Intervening on a predictor is a different question entirely, requiring the causal-inference methods covered elsewhere in this book.
  • A plausible explanation is not proof the model is right. If a model has secretly learned to read the scanner artefact, SHAP will faithfully and confidently report the scanner-related variable as important. The explanation is “correct” about the model and yet exposes that the model is wrong. Explanations are a tool for catching such failures — but only if you scrutinise them against clinical knowledge rather than rationalising whatever they show.
  • Explanations can be unstable. Re-run LIME, or compute SHAP on a different background sample, and the numbers can shift. Treat explanations as estimates with their own uncertainty, not as exact readouts.
  • Correlated features split the credit. When two variables carry overlapping information (say, weight and BMI), the model can use either, and the importance or SHAP credit gets divided between them in ways that can look arbitrary. A variable showing “low importance” may simply have had its thunder stolen by a correlated twin — not be genuinely irrelevant.

The honest stance is this: explanations are an indispensable tool for building trust, satisfying reporting standards, and — most valuably — catching a model that is right for the wrong reasons. They are not a substitute for external validation, for clinical judgement, or for the causal questions that ultimately matter to patients.

17.8 Summary

  • Accurate tree-ensemble models (random forests, XGBoost) are black boxes; explainability tools open them after training so we can see what they learned.
  • Global explanations describe the model overall; local explanations justify a single patient’s prediction. You need both.
  • Permutation importance ranks variables by how much shuffling each one hurts model performance — the cheapest check that the model relies on sensible variables.
  • Partial dependence plots show the average shape of one variable’s effect; switch to ALE when predictors are correlated.
  • SHAP is the modern standard: it fairly allocates each prediction among the variables, gives an exact additive local explanation (waterfall/force plots), and aggregates to a global view (beeswarm summary). For tree models it is fast and exact (TreeSHAP).
  • LIME is the older local method — a simple surrogate fitted near one patient — now largely superseded by SHAP but still seen in the literature.
  • Explanations reveal association learned by the model, not causation, and a plausible explanation is not proof the model is correct. Their greatest value is catching a model that predicts well for the wrong reason.
TipKey Takeaways
  • A prediction you cannot explain is a clinical liability. Explainability supports trust, fairness, and the TRIPOD+AI reporting expectation.
  • Distinguish global (“what drives the model?”) from local (“why this patient?”).
  • Use permutation importance for a quick global sanity check, PDP/ALE for the shape of effects, and SHAP as your primary tool for both global summaries (beeswarm) and per-patient explanations (waterfall).
  • Always sanity-check explanations against clinical knowledge; an unexpected top variable may signal a data leak or a proxy, not a finding.
  • Explanations are not causal. Never read a SHAP value or importance score as an intervention effect.

# Explaining Black-Box Models: Interpretability for Tabular Clinical Data {#sec-ml-explainability}

A random forest or an XGBoost model will often out-predict your trusty logistic regression. That is the good news. The bad news is that when a colleague, a patient, or a regulator asks *"why did the model say this patient is high risk?"*, you may have no answer. The model is a **black box**: data goes in, a number comes out, and the hundreds of trees that produced it are far too tangled to read by eye. This chapter is about prising that box open --- not by abandoning the accurate model, but by using a separate set of tools that interrogate it after it has been trained and tell us, in human terms, what it learned and why it made a particular prediction.

## Introduction

In the previous chapters you built tree ensembles that capture non-linear effects and interactions automatically, and you learned how to measure whether their predictions are any good. But a model that predicts well is not the same as a model you can trust. Consider why a clinician should care about *explaining* a model, not just scoring it:

- **Trust and adoption.** A clinician will not act on a risk score they cannot reconcile with their own reasoning. If the model flags a patient as high risk, the natural next question is "on what grounds?" --- and "the algorithm said so" is not an acceptable answer at the bedside. The case that explainability is a precondition for clinicians to accept AI tools, and the practical forms it can take, is set out for a medical audience by Reddy [@reddy2022explainability].
- **Catching the model relying on the wrong thing.** Models latch onto whatever predicts the outcome in the training data, even when it is clinically nonsensical. A famous class of failures: a model appears to predict pneumonia severity brilliantly, but it has really learned to read which *hospital scanner* took the X-ray (sicker patients went to a particular machine), or it keys on a code that is a **proxy for access to care** rather than for the disease itself. The model is right for the wrong reason, and it will fail the moment it meets a new hospital.
- **Fairness.** Explanations help reveal whether a model leans on variables (or proxies for them) that would make it perform unequally across groups of patients.
- **Regulation and reporting.** The TRIPOD+AI reporting guideline [@collins2024tripodai], published in 2024, now expects authors of clinical prediction models to describe how the model can be interpreted. Explainability has moved from a nice-to-have to an expectation.

::: {.callout-note}
## Why a clinician should care
An accurate prediction you cannot explain is a clinical liability, not an asset. The first time a black-box model confidently makes an indefensible recommendation --- and you cannot say why --- trust in the whole tool collapses. Explainability is how you find the model's blind spots *before* a patient does.
:::

A crucial distinction runs through this entire chapter, so we set it out at the start.

- **Global explanations** answer: *"What drives the model overall, across all patients?"* These tell you which variables the model relies on most and in which direction, summarising the model's general behaviour.
- **Local explanations** answer: *"Why did the model give **this** patient **this** prediction?"* These break a single prediction down into the contribution of each of that patient's characteristics.

Both are needed. A global view tells you the model broadly behaves sensibly; a local view lets you justify the number in front of an individual patient. @fig-explain-taxonomy lays out the methods we will cover and where each one sits.

One warning before we start, which we will return to in force at the end: every method in this chapter explains **the associations the model learned**, not the biology of disease. An explanation tells you how the model is behaving. It does not tell you that the model is *correct*, and it certainly does not establish *cause and effect*. ## Permutation Feature Importance (Global)

The simplest global question is: *which variables does the model actually need?* **Permutation feature importance** answers it with a beautifully direct idea. Take your trained model and a dataset where you know the true outcomes. Now pick one variable --- say, *prior admissions* --- and **shuffle its values** randomly between patients, so that each patient now has someone else's number of prior admissions. This deliberately destroys any real relationship between that variable and the outcome, while leaving every other variable untouched. Re-score the model on this scrambled data. If performance (say, the area under the ROC curve, or AUC) **drops a lot**, the model was leaning heavily on that variable --- it mattered. If performance barely changes, the model was not really using it.

The word "permutation" simply means shuffling. We repeat the shuffle a few times for each variable to average out the randomness, and we do this for every variable in turn. The result is a ranked list: the variables whose shuffling hurts the model most are the most important to it.

This is the recommended general-purpose importance measure because it asks the right question --- *how much does the model's accuracy depend on this variable?* --- and it works for any model, not just trees. (It is more honest than the built-in "impurity" importance you met in the trees chapter, which can be biased toward variables with many distinct values.)

    "num_comorbidities": rng.poisson(3, n),

# --- Fit a random forest ---
rf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=500, random_state=42, n_jobs=-1)
rf.fit(X, y)

# --- Permutation importance (shuffle each column, watch AUC fall) ---
result = permutation_importance(rf, X, y, scoring="roc_auc",
                                n_repeats=10, random_state=1)
order = result.importances_mean.argsort()
plt.barh(X.columns[order], result.importances_mean[order], color="#2E86AB")
plt.xlabel("Mean drop in AUC when the variable is shuffled")
plt.title("Permutation feature importance")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

**What the output shows.** You will see a horizontal bar chart with one bar per variable, sorted from most to least important. If, instead, the longest bar had been something clinically irrelevant (a patient ID, an admission timestamp, the name of the discharging ward), that would be a red flag that the model has latched onto a quirk of *how the data were collected* rather than the disease process. Permutation importance is your first, cheapest check that the model is paying attention to the right things.

## Partial Dependence Plots (Global)

Knowing that *age* matters does not tell you *how* it matters --- does risk climb steadily with age, jump only after 75, or even fall again in the very old? A **partial dependence plot (PDP)** answers the "how" by showing the average shape of the relationship the model learned between one variable and its prediction.

The idea, in plain terms: to see how the model's prediction depends on age, take your whole dataset and set *everybody's* age to 50, then ask the model for the average predicted risk. Now set everybody's age to 51, average again. Repeat across the full range of ages. Plot the average predicted risk against age, and you have traced out the relationship the model uses, holding the mix of all other variables fixed at their real values. The line might be flat (age does not move the prediction), steadily rising (more age, more risk), or curved (risk accelerates past a threshold) --- and crucially, you did not have to tell the model to look for any of those shapes; the PDP reveals whatever the model discovered on its own.

::: {.callout-warning}
## When predictors are correlated, prefer ALE
The PDP has a known weakness. When you force everybody's age to 90 while leaving their other variables untouched, you create *impossible patients* --- a 90-year-old with the bloodwork of a 40-year-old --- and the model is asked to predict for combinations it never saw. When predictors are strongly correlated (as clinical variables usually are), this can distort the curve.

**Accumulated Local Effects (ALE)** plots fix this. Instead of forcing unrealistic values on everyone, ALE looks at how the prediction *changes* over small, realistic windows of the variable and accumulates those local changes. The result reads almost exactly like a PDP --- the prediction on the y-axis, the variable on the x-axis --- but it stays honest in the presence of correlation. When in doubt with correlated clinical predictors, plot the ALE. In R, `iml` and `DALEX` both compute ALE; in Python, the `PyALE` package does.
:::

## SHAP: Shapley Values (Local and Global)

SHAP is the modern standard for explaining tabular models, so we will spend the most time here. The name stands for **SHapley Additive exPlanations** [@lundberg2017shap], and its core idea is borrowed from cooperative game theory.

**The intuition.** Imagine a prediction is a "payout" that a team of players earned together, where the players are the patient's variables (age, prior admissions, and so on). We want to fairly divide the credit for the prediction among the variables: how much did *this patient's* age push the predicted risk up or down, compared with an average patient? In the 1950s, the economist Lloyd Shapley solved exactly this fair-division problem for games. His solution --- the **Shapley value** --- considers every possible order in which players could join the team and averages each player's marginal contribution. It is the unique way of splitting the credit that satisfies a set of common-sense fairness rules (for instance, a variable that never changes the prediction gets zero credit). SHAP applies this idea to model predictions.

The payoff of all that theory is a property that makes SHAP uniquely useful: the SHAP values for one patient **add up**. Start from the model's average prediction across everyone (the "baseline"), add each variable's SHAP value for this patient, and you arrive exactly at this patient's prediction. So SHAP gives you a complete, additive accounting of every individual prediction --- nothing is left unexplained. Because each explanation is exact and additive, you can also average the *magnitudes* of SHAP values across many patients to get a trustworthy **global** importance ranking. This is why SHAP bridges the local and global worlds (recall @fig-explain-taxonomy).

For tree ensembles there is a fast, exact algorithm called **TreeSHAP**, so computing SHAP for a random forest or XGBoost model on a clinical dataset is quick.

### The SHAP summary (beeswarm) plot --- global

The single most informative SHAP figure is the **beeswarm summary plot**, and it repays careful reading. Picture this: each variable gets one horizontal row, and the rows are stacked with the most important variable at the top. Within a row, there is **one dot per patient**. The dot's horizontal position is that patient's SHAP value for that variable --- to the right means this variable *pushed that patient's predicted risk up*, to the left means it *pushed it down*, and the further from the centre, the bigger the push. The dot's **colour** encodes the variable's value for that patient: typically red/high to blue/low.

So a single beeswarm row tells you both *how important* a variable is (how spread out the dots are) and *which direction* it acts (do high values, in red, sit on the right or the left?). For *prior admissions*, you would expect to see red dots (many prior admissions) clustered on the right (pushing risk up) and blue dots (few prior admissions) on the left (pushing risk down) --- a clean, clinically sensible gradient. If a variable's colours were scrambled with no clear left-right pattern, the model is using it in a complicated, non-monotonic way worth investigating.

### SHAP dependence (scatter) plots --- global

A **SHAP dependence plot** zooms into one variable. It plots, for every patient, the variable's value on the x-axis against its SHAP value on the y-axis. This is like a PDP but built from the exact per-patient contributions, and it reveals the *shape* of the variable's effect plus the spread caused by interactions with other variables (vertical scatter at a given x-value means the variable's effect depends on something else).

### Explaining one patient --- local

For a single patient, the **waterfall plot** (or the equivalent **force plot**) is the clinician's friend. It starts at the baseline (the average predicted risk) at the bottom and stacks one bar per variable, each pushing the running total up (red, risk-increasing) or down (blue, risk-decreasing), until it lands on this patient's final predicted risk at the top. Read top to bottom, it is a plain sentence: *"This patient's risk is high mainly because they have four prior admissions (+12%) and a low discharge haemoglobin (+4%), partly offset by their relatively young age (−3%)."* That is exactly the kind of justification you can put in a note or discuss with a patient.

::: {.panel-tabset}

```{python}

#| eval: false

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import xgboost as xgb
import shap                # the canonical SHAP library
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- Re-create data and fit an XGBoost model inside this chunk ---
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 1500
X = pd.DataFrame({
    "age":               rng.normal(68, 12, n),
    "length_of_stay":    rng.poisson(5, n) + 1,
    "num_comorbidities": rng.poisson(3, n),
    "prior_admissions":  rng.poisson(1, n),
    "discharge_hb":      rng.normal(11, 2, n),
    "discharge_creat":   rng.lognormal(0.2, 0.5, n),
})
lin = (-3 + 0.45 * X["prior_admissions"] + 0.20 * X["num_comorbidities"]
       + 0.015 * (X["age"] - 68) - 0.05 * X["discharge_hb"])
y = rng.binomial(1, 1 / (1 + np.exp(-lin)))

model = xgb.XGBClassifier(n_estimators=100, max_depth=4, learning_rate=0.1,
                          eval_metric="logloss")
model.fit(X, y)

# --- Compute SHAP values (TreeExplainer = fast exact TreeSHAP) ---
explainer = shap.Explainer(model, X)
shap_values = explainer(X)

# GLOBAL: beeswarm summary plot
shap.plots.beeswarm(shap_values, show=True)

# GLOBAL: dependence (scatter) plot for prior admissions
shap.plots.scatter(shap_values[:, "prior_admissions"], show=True)

# LOCAL: explain a single patient (row 0) with a waterfall plot
shap.plots.waterfall(shap_values[0], show=True)
```

:::

**What the output shows.** Three figures, mapping onto the three SHAP uses.

1. The **beeswarm** is your global summary. Variables are ranked top-to-bottom by overall importance, with *prior admissions* and *num\_comorbidities* expected at the top. In each row, look at where the red (high-value) dots sit: for prior admissions they should lie to the right, confirming that *more prior admissions push predicted risk up*. This one plot replaces and enriches the permutation bar chart --- it shows importance *and* direction at once.
2. The **dependence/scatter plot** for prior admissions shows each patient as a dot, with the variable's value across the bottom and its SHAP contribution up the side. An upward trend confirms the risk-increasing relationship; any vertical spread hints that the effect is modulated by other variables (an interaction).
3. The **waterfall** explains one chosen patient. You will see the baseline risk at one end and the patient's final predicted risk at the other, with coloured bars in between showing exactly which of *their* characteristics raised or lowered the prediction and by how much. This is the figure to show when someone asks "why is *this* patient flagged?"

Taken together, SHAP lets you audit the model globally and defend any single prediction locally, using one coherent, additive accounting. That combination is why it has become the default tool. For a concrete clinical example, Lundberg and colleagues used SHAP to explain a model that predicts intraoperative hypoxaemia in real time, and showed that surfacing the per-patient risk factors to anaesthetists improved their ability to anticipate events --- a demonstration that local explanations can change behaviour at the bedside, not just on paper [@lundberg2018hypoxaemia]. A practical note: TreeSHAP is exact and fast for the tree models above; for other model types SHAP falls back to slower approximations (`KernelExplainer` in Python, `kernelshap` in R), so on large datasets you may explain a representative sample of patients rather than all of them.

## LIME (Local)

**LIME** --- Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations [@ribeiro2016lime] --- predates SHAP and is worth knowing because you will still see it in papers. Its idea is intuitive. To explain one patient's prediction, LIME creates a cloud of slightly altered versions of that patient (nudging variables up and down), asks the black-box model to score all of them, and then fits a *simple, transparent model* --- usually a small linear regression --- to that local cloud. Because the simple model only has to be accurate *near this one patient*, its coefficients give a readable, local approximation: "in the neighbourhood of this patient, higher creatinine and more prior admissions are what drive the risk up."

In practice LIME has largely been superseded by SHAP for tabular clinical data, because SHAP rests on firmer theory (the fairness guarantees of Shapley values) and its local explanations add up exactly to the prediction, whereas LIME's depend on somewhat arbitrary choices (how big is the neighbourhood? how is "nearby" defined?) that can make explanations unstable. Reach for SHAP first; understand LIME so you can read older literature and appreciate the shared "explain locally with a simple surrogate" idea.

## A Health Warning: What Explanations Do and Do Not Tell You

This is the most important section in the chapter. The tools above are powerful, and precisely because their output looks so authoritative, they are easy to over-trust. Ghassemi, Oakden-Rayner, and Beam make this case forcefully in *The Lancet Digital Health*, arguing that current explainability methods offer a "false hope" for patient-level decision support: an explanation can describe how the model behaves without ever assuring you that it reached its decision for a clinically valid reason, and they urge that rigorous internal and external validation --- not explanation --- is the more direct route to a trustworthy model [@ghassemi2021explainable]. Read that paper alongside this section.

::: {.callout-important}
## Explanations show association, not causation --- and not correctness
- **They describe the model, not the disease.** A SHAP plot says "the model uses high creatinine to raise predicted risk." It does *not* say "high creatinine causes readmission," and it certainly does not license "lowering creatinine will prevent readmission." The variable may be a proxy for sicker patients. **Never read an importance ranking or a SHAP plot as a causal effect.** Intervening on a predictor is a different question entirely, requiring the causal-inference methods covered elsewhere in this book.
- **A plausible explanation is not proof the model is right.** If a model has secretly learned to read the scanner artefact, SHAP will faithfully and confidently report *the scanner-related variable* as important. The explanation is "correct" about the model and yet exposes that the model is wrong. Explanations are a tool for *catching* such failures --- but only if you scrutinise them against clinical knowledge rather than rationalising whatever they show.
- **Explanations can be unstable.** Re-run LIME, or compute SHAP on a different background sample, and the numbers can shift. Treat explanations as estimates with their own uncertainty, not as exact readouts.
- **Correlated features split the credit.** When two variables carry overlapping information (say, weight and BMI), the model can use either, and the importance or SHAP credit gets divided between them in ways that can look arbitrary. A variable showing "low importance" may simply have had its thunder stolen by a correlated twin --- not be genuinely irrelevant.
:::

The honest stance is this: explanations are an indispensable tool for building trust, satisfying reporting standards, and --- most valuably --- *catching a model that is right for the wrong reasons*. They are not a substitute for external validation, for clinical judgement, or for the causal questions that ultimately matter to patients.

## Summary

- Accurate tree-ensemble models (random forests, XGBoost) are **black boxes**; explainability tools open them after training so we can see what they learned.
- **Global** explanations describe the model overall; **local** explanations justify a single patient's prediction. You need both.
- **Permutation importance** ranks variables by how much shuffling each one hurts model performance --- the cheapest check that the model relies on sensible variables.
- **Partial dependence plots** show the average shape of one variable's effect; switch to **ALE** when predictors are correlated.
- **SHAP** is the modern standard: it fairly allocates each prediction among the variables, gives an exact additive local explanation (waterfall/force plots), and aggregates to a global view (beeswarm summary). For tree models it is fast and exact (TreeSHAP).
- **LIME** is the older local method --- a simple surrogate fitted near one patient --- now largely superseded by SHAP but still seen in the literature.
- Explanations reveal **association learned by the model, not causation**, and a plausible explanation is **not proof the model is correct**. Their greatest value is catching a model that predicts well for the wrong reason.

::: {.callout-tip}
## Key Takeaways
- A prediction you cannot explain is a clinical liability. Explainability supports trust, fairness, and the TRIPOD+AI reporting expectation.
- Distinguish **global** ("what drives the model?") from **local** ("why this patient?").
- Use **permutation importance** for a quick global sanity check, **PDP/ALE** for the shape of effects, and **SHAP** as your primary tool for both global summaries (beeswarm) and per-patient explanations (waterfall).
- Always sanity-check explanations against clinical knowledge; an unexpected top variable may signal a data leak or a proxy, not a finding.
- Explanations are **not causal**. Never read a SHAP value or importance score as an intervention effect.
:::

## References and Further Reading

The Shapley-value approach to model explanation was introduced by Lundberg and Lee (2017), "A Unified Approach to Interpreting Model Predictions" [@lundberg2017shap], presented at NeurIPS; this paper unified several earlier local explanation methods under the SHAP framework and is the canonical reference. The fast, exact TreeSHAP algorithm for tree ensembles was developed in follow-up work by the same group. For LIME, the original paper is Ribeiro, Singh, and Guestrin (2016), "'Why Should I Trust You?': Explaining the Predictions of Any Classifier" [@ribeiro2016lime], presented at KDD 2016.

For a thorough, freely available and very readable book-length treatment of the whole field --- permutation importance, PDP, ALE, LIME, SHAP, and their pitfalls --- see Christoph Molnar's *Interpretable Machine Learning: A Guide for Making Black Box Models Explainable* [@molnar2022interpretable]. It is the single best next read after this chapter. The companion methodology for the R ecosystem is described by Biecek and Burzykowski in *Explanatory Model Analysis* [@biecek2021ema], which underpins the `DALEX` package; Biecek's earlier paper [@biecek2018dalex] introduced `DALEX` itself.

The tree-ensemble models being explained here are covered in the previous chapter: random forests follow Breiman (2001) [@breiman2001random] and gradient boosting follows the XGBoost system of Chen and Guestrin (2016) [@chen2016xgboost]. For the clinician's perspective on why and how models should be explained, Reddy (2022) [@reddy2022explainability] gives a short, accessible overview in *The Lancet Digital Health*, and Lundberg et al. (2018) [@lundberg2018hypoxaemia] in *Nature Biomedical Engineering* is an influential worked example of SHAP applied to a real anaesthesia prediction task. The essential counterweight is Ghassemi, Oakden-Rayner, and Beam (2021), "The false hope of current approaches to explainable artificial intelligence in health care" [@ghassemi2021explainable], required reading on the limits of explainability for patient-level decisions. Finally, the reporting context for all of this is the TRIPOD+AI statement [@collins2024tripodai], which sets out current expectations for describing how a clinical prediction model can be interpreted. In the R ecosystem, the `shapviz`, `kernelshap`, `fastshap`, `treeshap`, `iml`, `pdp`, and `vip` packages implement these methods; in Python, the `shap` library and scikit-learn's `sklearn.inspection` module (`permutation_importance`, `PartialDependenceDisplay`) are the standard tools.
